The models keep getting better, and the agents built on top of them keep dying in prototype. If you have spent the last two years anywhere near an enterprise that tried to put an autonomous agent into production, you have watched this happen at least once, and you have probably watched it happen quietly, because failed prototypes do not get postmortems. They get renamed, deprioritised, and forgotten.

There is a number that gets passed around to describe this, and the number is ninety-five percent — ninety-five out of every hundred enterprise agent projects never surviving contact with production. I have repeated that number myself, so let me hold it to the standard that everything else in this series will be held to: I did not collect it, I cannot trace its sample or its methodology, and I will not pretend otherwise. Treat it as an industry folk statistic, flagged as such. What I can offer you instead is the pattern I have observed directly, which requires no survey to verify because you can perform the autopsy on any dead agent project you have access to. The model is almost never the cause of death. The cause of death is everything around the model. Nobody designed the tools the agent calls, so the agent wastes half of its context window guessing at them. Nobody versioned the prompts, so nobody can say which change broke the behaviour. Nobody decided the point at which a human being gets to veto an irreversible action, so either everything requires approval and the reviewer stops reading, or nothing does and the first bad day becomes a public incident. And nobody wrote a single evaluation, so nobody can distinguish a regression from bad luck. The project does not fail loudly. It runs on hope and a demonstration dataset until the hope runs out.

Here is the observation that took me an embarrassingly long time to accept: the skills required to prevent that death are not a menu from which you may choose freely. They form a dependency graph, and the graph has an order. You cannot debug an agent loop that will not terminate if you cannot read an inference bill, because the loop is a cost problem before it is a logic problem. You cannot read an inference bill if you do not understand what a key-value cache — the memory structure that makes a language model fast, and the thing that quietly consumes your hardware — is doing underneath you. Knowledge of this field is one continuous stack, from silicon to strategy, and most engineers walk it in exactly the wrong direction. They spend six months on transformer mathematics, which has become table stakes, and then improvise the layers where projects actually die.

So I am going to walk the entire graph, in order, in public. Beginning this Sunday, I will publish two posts every day for one hundred weeks — a morning post that teaches and an evening post that argues. The plan covers ten pillars, from classical system design fundamentals at the bottom, up through the internals of large language models, post-training and alignment, inference and edge deployment, harness engineering, loop and graph engineering, evaluation and governance, the operational substrate beneath machine learning, production case studies, and finally career leverage for forward-deployed engineering, which is the emerging role that sits closest to where these systems succeed or fail. The weekly rhythm never changes: a kickoff on Sunday, a deep explanation on Monday, a walk through real code on Tuesday, a case study on Wednesday, a hands-on exercise on Thursday, a deliberate argument on Friday in which the opposing position is presented fairly before it is attacked, and a recap with a quiz on Saturday.

Because a series about engineering discipline should be able to survive its own standards, this one runs under five commitments. First, everything is pre-registered: every one of the one thousand four hundred and six planned posts was written down and committed to version control before this first one went out, so if I quietly rewrite the plan to look wiser in hindsight, the history will contradict me. Second, nothing is cited unverified: all twenty-nine repositories backing this series were checked on the twentieth of August against the GitHub API — checked that they exist, that they are maintained, and that their contents actually say what people claim they say. One of them turned out to have been removed from GitHub under a copyright takedown, and it was retired from the reading list with a note rather than silently replaced, because dead sources deserve burials, not cover-ups. Third, every claim carries its uncertainty, and every number names its source in the sentence that uses it or admits that it cannot — you watched me apply that rule to my own favourite statistic three paragraphs ago. Fourth, corrections are content: when something I teach turns out to be wrong, the correction will be published as its own post rather than edited silently into the archive. Fifth, the plan updates on evidence, which brings me to this evening.

At five o'clock today I will publish the first poll of this series, and I want you to see it coming. In it, I will state openly what I currently believe you need most from these hundred weeks — a belief I have formed from watching agent projects die and engineers get hired, and a belief I have never actually measured. The poll is the instrument that measures it. The second pass of this series will be re-weighted on the results, and when that happens I will publish the tally beside the revised calendar so that you can verify exactly how much your vote moved it. A belief you refuse to measure is a belief you are protecting. This evening, you get to break one of mine.

Week one opens on Sunday morning with system design fundamentals, beginning with the CAP theorem, from first principles. Follow along or do not — but if your agent dies in prototype next quarter, you will at least know which layer it died in.
